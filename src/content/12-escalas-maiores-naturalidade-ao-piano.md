Several keyboard players have told me they feel comfortable in C major but struggle when a song moves to a key with more black keys. F♯ major or A♭ major can feel intimidating when your hands do not yet recognize the patterns.

The keyboard's **transpose** function offers a shortcut. It shifts the pitch of what you play without changing the keys under your fingers.

For example:

- To play a C-major fingering while sounding in D major, set transpose to **+2 semitones**.
- To play a G-major fingering while sounding in G♭ major, set transpose to **−1 semitone**.

That can be useful on stage. It also becomes awkward when a song changes key and you have to keep adjusting the setting. Learning the scales gives you another option: move your hands directly to the new key.

## Why start with the twelve major scales?

A major scale has seven distinct notes, arranged in this interval pattern:

**whole – whole – half – whole – whole – whole – half**

The same pattern can begin on any of the twelve pitch classes. Practicing those twelve starting points helps you recognize keys, understand chord relationships, and become more comfortable with the keyboard's layout.

That was the purpose of my ebook: a practical, clearly organized guide to the twelve major scales and their fingerings.

Scales are a much larger subject than this one guide. There are minor scales, modes, and many other patterns to explore. I chose the major scales because they appear so often in the music we play.

## Learn the fingerings as well as the notes

Knowing a scale on paper is useful, but it does not automatically make your hands comfortable playing it. Practice the right and left hands separately, then combine them slowly.

Notice where the thumbs cross and how the black keys affect the fingering. The pattern is not identical in every key or in both hands.

The original article used the Pareto principle as an analogy for focused practice. It was encouragement to work on a useful foundation, rather than a measured claim that twelve scales deliver a fixed percentage of musical skill.
